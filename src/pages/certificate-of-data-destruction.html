What a certificate of data destruction should show

It's your written record of what happened to the drives in your retired computers. Here's what a useful one lists, and why each line matters.

See our sample certificate ↗

What it should list

Every device

Each computer by make, model, serial number and your asset tag, so it matches your inventory line for line.

Every drive

Each drive by its own serial number, tied to the computer it came out of.

How it was handled

Shredded or erased, drive by drive. Not every line has to say destroyed, but every line has to say what happened.

When

The pickup date and the date the drives were shredded or erased.

Who

Who checked the drives, who shredded them, and the recycler's receipt number for the shredded ones.

A signature

Signed by the company that took responsibility for the equipment, with contact details you can use later.

Why serial numbers matter

A certificate that says "12 hard drives destroyed" can't be matched to anything. One that lists each drive by serial number lets you close out each machine in your asset records and answer a client, an insurer or an auditor who asks where a specific laptop went.

Shredded or erased?

Most desktops and older laptops have a drive that comes out, and it can be shredded. MacBooks and many thin laptops have storage built into the board. That storage is erased with the maker's own erase function, so the machine can be reused. If your policy requires physical destruction, say so up front, and those devices go to the recycler whole. Either way, the certificate should say which was done.

It's the second half of the record

The first half is a signed transfer at pickup that lists every machine. Together they show what left your office, when, and what happened to each drive. Keep both with your asset records, for as long as your own record-keeping policy says.
